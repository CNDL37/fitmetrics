Step on a bathroom scale and it gives you a single number that lumps together muscle, bone, water, and every kind of fat your body carries. But not all body fat behaves the same way. The fat just under your skin — **subcutaneous fat**, the kind you can pinch — is largely inert. The fat packed deep in your abdomen, wrapped around your liver, pancreas, and intestines, is something else entirely. This is **visceral fat**, and gram for gram it is far more dangerous to your long-term health.

## What makes visceral fat different

Visceral fat is not a passive storage depot. It is metabolically active tissue that releases a steady stream of free fatty acids and inflammatory signaling molecules directly into the portal vein — the blood supply that drains straight into your liver. That gives it an outsized influence on how your whole body handles energy.

Three mechanisms make it especially harmful:

- **Inflammation.** Visceral fat secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines that promote a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation linked to cardiovascular disease and many cancers.
- **Insulin resistance.** The free fatty acids it dumps into the liver interfere with insulin signaling, pushing the body toward the high blood sugar and high insulin levels that precede type 2 diabetes.
- **Adverse lipids.** It is associated with higher triglycerides and lower HDL ("good") cholesterol — a pattern strongly tied to heart disease.

Because of these effects, excess visceral fat is a central feature of **metabolic syndrome**, the cluster of risk factors that dramatically raises the odds of heart attack, stroke, and diabetes.

## Why "skinny" doesn't mean safe

One of the most counterintuitive findings in metabolic health is that you can have a normal body weight and a normal body mass index and still carry a risky amount of visceral fat. Researchers sometimes describe this as being "metabolically obese, normal weight." These individuals look lean but have the internal fat distribution — and the elevated cardiometabolic risk — of someone heavier.

The reverse is also true. Some people carry substantial subcutaneous fat, particularly on the hips and thighs, while keeping visceral fat low. Their BMI may flag them as overweight, yet their metabolic risk is comparatively modest. This is exactly why BMI alone is a crude screening tool, and why where you carry fat matters as much as how much you carry.

## How to estimate visceral fat without a scan

The gold standard for measuring visceral fat is imaging — a CT or MRI scan — which is expensive and impractical for routine use. Fortunately, simple tape-measure ratios are strong proxies for abdominal fat because visceral fat drives waistline size.

**Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR)** is the most useful single number. Divide your waist circumference by your height in the same units. A practical rule of thumb popularized by researchers is to *keep your waist to less than half your height* — a ratio under 0.5. Ratios climbing toward 0.6 and beyond signal progressively higher cardiometabolic risk. WHtR has the advantage of working across different heights and body types better than waist circumference alone. (Our [guide to interpreting your waist-to-height ratio](/blog/how-to-interpret-whtr/) goes deeper.)

**Waist circumference** on its own is still informative. General thresholds for elevated risk are roughly 40 inches (102 cm) for men and 35 inches (88 cm) for women, with lower thresholds for some populations.

**Waist-to-hip ratio** captures fat distribution — an "apple" versus "pear" shape — and complements WHtR.

To measure your waist correctly, place the tape midway between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hip bones, breathe out normally, and don't compress the skin. Take the reading at the same point each time. Our [how-to guide on body circumference measurement](/blog/how-to-measure-body-circumference/) covers the technique in detail.

## What actually reduces visceral fat

The encouraging news is that visceral fat is often the *first* fat to respond when you adopt healthier habits — it tends to be metabolically mobile. Evidence-based levers include:

- **Regular aerobic exercise.** Sustained moderate activity, including lower-intensity "Zone 2" work, is particularly effective at mobilizing visceral fat. Most guidelines target at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week.
- **Resistance training.** Building and preserving muscle improves insulin sensitivity and supports a healthier body composition over time.
- **A modest, sustainable calorie deficit.** You cannot spot-reduce belly fat with crunches, but an overall energy deficit preferentially trims visceral stores early.
- **Limiting refined carbohydrates, added sugars, and alcohol**, all of which are linked to greater visceral accumulation.
- **Adequate sleep and stress management.** Short sleep and chronically elevated cortisol both promote central fat deposition.

There is no supplement, wrap, or targeted gadget that selectively removes visceral fat. The combination of consistent movement, resistance training, sensible nutrition, and sleep is what reliably works.

## The bottom line

The number on your scale tells you almost nothing about the fat that matters most. Visceral fat is the metabolically dangerous tissue around your organs, and it can be elevated even when your weight looks fine. A tape measure and your waist-to-height ratio give you a far better read on your cardiometabolic risk than weight alone — and they cost nothing. Track that ratio over time, and you'll be watching the number that actually moves the needle on your health.
