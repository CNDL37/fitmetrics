If you could change one number to improve your odds of a long, healthy life, a strong candidate would be your **cardiorespiratory fitness** — your body's ability to take in, transport, and use oxygen during sustained exercise. Measured as **VO₂ max**, it turns out to be one of the most powerful predictors of mortality we have, and unlike many risk factors, it's one you can directly improve.

## What VO₂ max actually measures

VO₂ max is the maximum rate at which your body can consume oxygen during intense exercise, usually expressed in milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute (mL/kg/min). It reflects the whole oxygen-delivery chain working together: how hard your heart can pump, how well your blood carries oxygen, and how effectively your muscles' mitochondria extract and use it.

A higher VO₂ max means a more capable cardiovascular system and more metabolically efficient muscles. It's the physiological definition of "aerobically fit."

## The link to lifespan

Large studies tracking thousands of people over many years have found a strong, graded relationship between cardiorespiratory fitness and the risk of dying — from heart disease, from cancer, and from all causes combined. The pattern is striking in a few ways:

- **The relationship is dose-dependent.** Each step up in fitness is associated with lower mortality risk. There is no clear point where additional fitness stops helping.
- **The biggest gains are at the bottom.** Moving from the *least fit* category to even *below-average* fitness is associated with a dramatic drop in risk — often the single largest jump. You don't have to become an athlete to capture most of the benefit; you have to avoid being unfit.
- **The effect size is large.** In some analyses, low cardiorespiratory fitness carried a risk comparable to or greater than traditional factors like smoking, high blood pressure, or diabetes.

In other words, being sedentary and unfit is not a neutral baseline — it is itself a major, modifiable risk factor.

## Why fitness is so protective

Cardiorespiratory fitness is a kind of integrated report card for several systems at once. A strong VO₂ max generally reflects a healthy heart, flexible blood vessels, good blood pressure regulation, efficient mitochondria, and often a more active lifestyle overall. Training that raises it also tends to improve insulin sensitivity, lipids, body composition, and inflammation. Fitness isn't just a marker of health — the training that builds it actively produces health.

## You can change it

Here's the empowering part: VO₂ max is highly trainable at virtually any age. While genetics set part of your ceiling, the typical person can improve their cardiorespiratory fitness substantially with consistent training — and improvements in fitness over time are associated with reduced mortality risk, meaning it's not too late to benefit.

The most effective approach combines two ingredients:

**A base of moderate aerobic exercise.** The foundation is steady, sustainable activity — brisk walking, cycling, swimming, jogging. Much of this should be done at a comfortable, conversational intensity, the **Zone 2** range that builds mitochondrial density and aerobic efficiency. We cover this in depth in [why Zone 2 matters](/blog/why-zone-2-matters/). Guidelines recommend at least **150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week**, and more confers additional benefit.

**A smaller dose of higher-intensity work.** Briefly pushing close to your limit — intervals of harder effort — is particularly effective at raising VO₂ max specifically. Even one or two sessions per week of interval-style training, where you alternate hard efforts with recovery, can meaningfully move the number. These should be introduced gradually and, for anyone with cardiovascular risk factors, after a conversation with a clinician.

A practical weekly template for many people: most aerobic time at an easy, conversational pace, plus one or two shorter, harder sessions, alongside the [resistance training](/blog/strength-training-for-metabolic-health/) that protects muscle and bone.

## How to gauge your fitness

A true VO₂ max test happens in a lab with a mask and a treadmill, but you don't need one to track progress. Useful everyday proxies include:

- **How you handle daily exertion** — stairs, hills, carrying groceries — getting easier over time.
- **Resting heart rate** trending lower as fitness improves.
- **Heart rate recovery** — how quickly your pulse drops in the minute after hard effort — getting faster.
- Many wearables now estimate VO₂ max from heart rate and pace; the absolute number is approximate, but the *trend* is informative.

## The bottom line

Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest predictors of longevity we know of, and being unfit is a major, modifiable risk in its own right. The largest rewards come from simply moving from sedentary to active — you don't need to be elite. Build a base of easy aerobic activity, add a little higher-intensity work, pair it with strength training, and you'll be improving one of the few health numbers that reliably tracks with how long, and how well, you live.
